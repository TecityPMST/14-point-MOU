State of the MOU
Day 71 of a 60-day clock that expired eleven days ago, and the eighth refresh since expiry again records no change in any article’s status — counts hold at Breached 10, At Risk 4 — but the window carries the most substantive movement on the maritime file since the lapse, and it runs outside the memorandum rather than through it. Iran and Oman agreed a temporary joint corridor through the Strait of Hormuz, 7 miles wide, entered and partly exited through Iranian territorial waters, together with a joint mine-clearing project, technical talks toward a permanent route and mechanisms for information exchange, traffic management and security services; Oman’s Badr Albusaidi said he hoped to announce it soon. Three facts hold the status where it is: Gharibabadi said expressly that the strait is not open and that Washington must first implement the commitments it has violated, with the army adding that ships will pass only by Iranian permission; the IRGC said the parties had agreed each country’s share of the strait’s revenues, which cuts against a toll-free obligation that expired unused on 16 August; and nothing changed on the water, where UKMTO logged an oil tanker disabled by an unknown projectile nine miles northeast of Ash Shishah on 24 August — a strike the previous refresh missed and this one records. Diplomatically a second mediator entered inside four days: Qatar’s prime minister travels to Tehran today to discuss continued mediation, freedom of navigation and a return to the pre-28 February position, after Pakistan’s mission on 24 August. Both should be read against what the American principals said in the same window. Rubio told allied foreign ministers there would be no new strikes “for the time being” while the blockade and Treasury campaign tighten, with a second official putting the horizon at the midterm elections, and Trump told Al Jazeera he is “not in a hurry”. That makes the twenty-eighth night without strikes a posture rather than a ceasefire, and Iran’s army spokesman answered it by saying damaged systems had been rebuilt, new equipment imported and the doctrine changed from defensive to offensive. On the sanctions side the American register went quiet without softening: OFAC carried no Iran-related action dated 25–27 August, so the 24 August package stands and the major financial institution Treasury said it would designate this week had not been named. Iran took the fight to the UN instead, with Araghchi asking the Secretary-General to report on the humanitarian effect of the measures and asking member states not to implement them. Markets discounted the pressure: Brent fell 2.5 percent to $86.38 and WTI to $80.52, a third straight daily decline, while the rial hit an all-time low beyond 2,000,000 to the dollar and two south-western petrochemical plants shut to save electricity. Article 8 hardened without moving: Iran’s atomic-energy chief said for the first time on the record that the IAEA cannot inspect the strike-damaged sites until a wartime inspection protocol is agreed, which converts an inferred access gap into a stated condition while leaving the no-weapons commitment itself unbroken and unverifiable.
